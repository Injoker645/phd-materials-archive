When a doctor absorbs a hospital's unwritten habits over twenty years and calls the result clinical judgment, we usually trust it. Nobody asks, for a given decision, which part is medicine and which part is that hospital's documentation and discharge culture. When a model absorbs the same years of the same habits from notes and labels, we call it bias and demand an explanation before we trust the score. Same contamination. Same inability, on either side, to cleanly separate the two. So what are we asking the model to prove that we have never routinely asked the doctor to prove: a standard of evidence, or a standard of who is allowed to be unaccountable?

I want to spend a PhD building the missing measurement. For a clinical prediction model that outputs a score, not a citable paragraph: how much of *this* prediction rests on site-invariant clinical signal versus site-specific process. Existing tools mostly try to train the gap away in aggregate. On clinical multi-site benchmarks, those domain-generalisation tricks often fail to beat simply pooling data. Post-hoc “explanations” can also fail a faithfulness check. The gap I care about is narrower and more actionable: a falsifiable, per-prediction provenance signal, validated when the same model is tested under another site’s distribution, with a pre-registered check for prevalence shift across environments. If the invariant share is wrong, cross-site performance should expose it. The output should change a deployment decision (don’t deploy, recalibrate locally, escalate), not decorate a dashboard.

I grew up in a hospital compound where my father worked. That doesn’t make me a clinician. It did make hospital information systems feel concrete early: paper forms, inconsistent terminology, data that never reached the people who needed it. Later I wrote a Medium piece on historical underrepresentation in medical research and how those gaps get amplified when the same records train models. On day one of my master’s at Uppsala I contacted a bioethics researcher at BMC Uppsala; we attended an EU AI Act healthcare conference together and worked on a grant proposal framing around how people forgive human versus machine error in care. That question never left. It’s why I’m not looking for a pure theory PhD disconnected from deployment.

The technical path so far is adjacent, not identical, and I should say that plainly. My MSc thesis, *Cracks in the Feed*, studied harmful-comment evasion and detection on short-form video under the EU Digital Services Act: a 2,564-comment multi-platform corpus, a custom annotation schema, text and multimodal (GIF) detectors. Honest limits: single annotator; GIF test set n = 62. Different domain, but the habits transfer: data construction as a first-class problem, label uncertainty, multimodal fusion, and refusing to overclaim tiny evaluations. At Fyris AI I built an emergency-department patient-demand forecasting model. In a short role at KFSH & RC I helped clean and define the data dictionary for a CSICU / procedures Power BI dashboard. In Uppsala’s Hospital Data Integration / IT & Society work I spent most of a cohort project on the human-research side of interoperability around Akademiska Sjukhuset. None of these are a US academic EHR lab. That’s the honest gap. What they are is repeated contact with clinical operations, messy data, and the difference between a model that fits a file and a model someone would actually use.

I trained as a software engineer at NUST (Gold Medal) and completed an MSc in Data Science (machine learning and statistics) at Uppsala. GRE 158V / 164Q exists if anyone cares; CPH states GRE scores are not considered, which is fine by me. IELTS 8.5. I left a Samsung innovation analyst role that looked good on paper because it was constrained down to not installing Python on a work laptop. I’d rather spend several years on a problem where the answer matters outside the university.

CPH is the right structure for this question because the programme is built to keep computation and clinical context in the same training loop, not as an optional side collaboration. I’m specifically interested in working with Irene Chen on auditing clinical ML under incomplete data and distribution shift; with Ziad Obermeyer on how proxy labels and institutional incentives warp what models learn; and with Ahmed Alaa on multimodal clinical representations and statistically valid prediction. The multi-site setting matters. Program documentation of the UC Health Data Warehouse (de-identified EHR across five UC medical centres) and UCSF Health / Information Commons clinical notes is exactly the kind of environment where “does this evidence travel?” can be tested without pretending one hospital’s notes are universal medicine. I am not claiming guaranteed access as an applicant. I am saying the research question only makes sense where multi-site clinical data and deployment partners exist, and CPH is organised around that.

I’m a Pakistani international student. I’m applying because the programme states five years of support including internationals who maintain satisfactory progress, and because the dual-campus design (Berkeley year one, rotations on both campuses, hybrid coursework, home campus later with the advisor) matches how I already work: move toward the data and the clinical partners, don’t wait for a perfect single-building lab.

I’d rather measure whether a score travels than invent another story about why it shouldn’t have to.
