I have lived in more than one healthcare system without ever training as a clinician. I grew up in Pakistan in a hospital compound where my father worked. I later lived in Saudi Arabia, and I am finishing a master's in Sweden. The constant across those moves was not a particular hospital, and it was not a particular model of care. It was the gap between what a system says it does and what you see when you watch people try to move information through it.

That is not a romantic origin story, and I do not want it read as one. As a child I was not analysing documentation practices. I was noticing that forms did not match, that the same thing had different names in different rooms, and that a lot of important information lived in someone's head rather than in a place another person could find it. When I eventually worked, briefly, on a procedures dashboard inside a hospital system, the adult version of that observation was waiting for me: the data dictionary is where the politics and the habits show up, not in the model card.

Moving for education also meant learning how differently "evidence" travels. A result that is obvious in one country's clinical culture is contested in another. A dataset that looks complete is complete only relative to who was counted. A few years ago I wrote about how historical underrepresentation in medical research gets amplified when those records become training data. I still think that is the right worry. What I have updated is the mechanism I care about most. The loud failures are demographic. The quiet ones are institutional: a model that performs well because it has learned one hospital's discharge rhythm, one department's note template, one decade's coding habits.

Sweden added a regulatory frame I did not have before. Studying under the EU Digital Services Act for my thesis, and sitting through healthcare sessions of the AI Act conversation with a bioethics researcher I contacted in my first week, made the accountability question concrete. When something goes wrong, who can actually answer for it, and what kind of answer counts. I do not have a settled position on that. I have a research direction that takes the question seriously enough to try to measure part of it.

I am applying as an international student who will need a visa, and who has already crossed enough systems to know that "generalisation" is not an abstract ML term for me. It is the difference between a tool that helps in the building where it was trained and a tool that still helps when the building, the staff, and the unwritten rules change. That is the personal reason I care about provenance in clinical models. The technical reason is in my Statement of Academic Purpose. They are the same reason from two sides.
